## Nestlé S.A. (NESN.SW / NSRGY) — Valuation Analysis

**On a forward earnings basis, Nestlé screens as moderately expensive relative to its stated industry group.** The forward P/E of 17.62–17.72x sits 23.1% above the reported Consumer Packaged Goods sector median of 14.32x, which on its face argues against a "cheap" read. Within the narrower peer set provided, however, Nestlé lands squarely in the middle: above Unilever's trailing P/E of 12.93x (itself described as 28% below Unilever's own 10-year median, i.e., historically cheap for Unilever) and below Danone's trailing P/E of 22.54x (described as near Danone's own 10-year median, i.e., in-line for Danone). So relative to this specific three-company set, Nestlé is neither the cheapest nor the most expensive — it's the middle case — even though it looks rich against the broader sector median. Those two comparisons (broad sector vs. named peers) point in somewhat different directions and the data doesn't reconcile them.

**A second, unresolved issue sits within Nestlé's own multiples.** The trailing P/E (28.53x) is roughly 62% higher than the forward P/E (17.62x). That gap is large enough to imply the market/analysts expect a substantial earnings rebound, and the data does note a -31.4% net income decline elsewhere — but the provided material does not explicitly state that this decline is the cause of the trailing/forward gap, so I can't confirm that link; it's a plausible but unverified inference from what's given.

**The DCF evidence is internally contradictory and should not be relied upon as presented.** One source puts intrinsic value at $113.62 against a stated current price of $77.76 (implying +46.1% upside); another puts intrinsic value at $179.01 against a stated current price of $102 (implying the stock is undervalued by roughly the same rough magnitude in percentage terms, per that source's framing). Both nominally point toward undervaluation, but the two "current price" reference points themselves ($77.76 vs. $102) disagree by about 31%, and the data does not explain whether this is due to mixing ADR (NSRGY) and ordinary share (NESN.SW) pricing, a currency conversion issue, or simply different collection dates. Because the baseline prices don't agree, the two DCF conclusions cannot be treated as independently corroborating each other — they may simply be restating the same directional signal on two different (and unreconciled) price bases. I would flag this as the most significant internal contradiction in the dataset.

**Currency and scale inconsistencies further limit confidence.** Market capitalization is given as both $258.56B and $385B (a ~1.5x discrepancy with no stated cause), and the broader dataset mixes CHF-denominated (NESN.SW) and USD-denominated (ADR) prices without supplying an exchange rate to reconcile them. Any cross-source price or ratio comparison here carries that unresolved currency ambiguity.

**Bottom line on the data as given:** the forward P/E signal (rich vs. sector median, mid-pack vs. named peers) is legible enough to state directionally, though the two peer comparisons don't fully agree with each other. The DCF signal is not usable as stated — the fair-value and reference-price figures conflict across sources badly enough that I would not characterize the stock as clearly cheap or expensive on a DCF basis without first resolving which currency/share-class/date each figure refers to. Given the market-cap discrepancy and the unexplained trailing/forward P/E gap, the dataset overall is insufficient to reach a single, confident valuation verdict — it supports only the narrower, hedged observations above.

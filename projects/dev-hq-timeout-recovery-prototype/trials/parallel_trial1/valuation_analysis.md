## Valuation Analysis: Nestlé S.A. (NESN.SW / NSRGY)

**On a relative multiples basis, Nestlé screens as moderately expensive.** Its forward P/E of 17.62–17.72 sits 23.1% above the Consumer Packaged Goods industry median forward P/E of 14.32, which on its face supports a "not cheap" read. Within its direct peer set the picture is more nuanced: Nestlé's forward P/E lands squarely between Unilever's trailing P/E of 12.93 (28% below Unilever's own 10-year median — the cheapest of the three on this measure) and Danone's trailing P/E of 22.54 (roughly in line with Danone's 10-year median — the most expensive of the three). So relative to its two closest packaged-food peers, Nestlé is priced in the middle, even as it trades at a premium to the broader industry median. Note also that this three-way comparison mixes trailing (Unilever, Danone) and forward (Nestlé) multiples, which is not a like-for-like comparison and should be read with that caveat.

**A separate contradiction sits within Nestlé's own multiples**: trailing P/E (28.53) is markedly higher than forward P/E (17.62–17.72) — a gap of roughly 11 points. The data provided does not explain this gap, including whether it reflects the market pricing in an earnings recovery from a reported net income decline of -31.4%, a low base-year comparison, or something else. Without that explanation, it's not possible to say whether the "cheap-looking" forward multiple is a genuine reflection of improving fundamentals or an artifact of a depressed trailing earnings base.

**The discounted cash flow (DCF) evidence is internally contradictory and cannot support a valuation judgment as given.** One source puts fair value at $113.62 against a stated current price of $77.76 (implying 46.1% upside); another puts fair value at $179.01 against a stated current price of $102 (implying 43% undervaluation). Beyond the fair-value estimates themselves differing by more than 50%, the two sources don't even agree on what the current price is ($77.76 vs. $102) — a discrepancy the data attributes possibly to ADR-vs-ordinary-share (NESN.SW) currency/price mixing or differing snapshot dates, but this is not confirmed anywhere in the material provided. Given that the "upside" percentages are calculated off two different and unreconciled base prices, neither DCF figure can be treated as a reliable, directly comparable estimate of intrinsic value versus market price.

**This currency/price inconsistency is systemic in the data, not isolated to the DCF.** The underlying material flags that CHF-denominated ordinary-share prices and USD-denominated ADR prices are used interchangeably across sources without an accompanying exchange rate, making it impossible to cross-check price-based figures (technical, valuation, sentiment) against one another with confidence. Separately, reported market capitalization also disagrees by roughly 1.5x across sources ($258.56B vs. $385B), with no explanation given — this alone should raise caution about relying on any single-source figure in this dataset without corroboration.

**Bottom line on the data as provided:** the multiples-based comparison (forward P/E vs. industry and peers) leans toward Nestlé looking moderately expensive versus the broader industry but mid-pack versus its two closest peers — though even that comparison mixes trailing and forward bases. The DCF evidence, meanwhile, is too internally inconsistent (divergent fair values built on divergent, unreconciled current prices, likely a currency or timing artifact) to be used as a corroborating or contradicting data point. Given the unresolved trailing/forward P/E gap, the unexplained market-cap discrepancy, and the unreconciled currency mixing across the dataset, the data is insufficient to reach a confident, single-answer judgment on whether Nestlé is cheap or expensive overall — it is more accurate to say the multiples-only view suggests a modest premium to industry (with peer positioning mixed), while the DCF-based view cannot be relied upon at all given the internal contradictions flagged above.

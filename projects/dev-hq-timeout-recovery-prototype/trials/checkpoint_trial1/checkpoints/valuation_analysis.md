## Nestlé S.A. (NESN.SW / NSRGY) — Valuation Read, Based Solely on Supplied Data

**Earnings multiples show a premium versus the stated industry benchmark, but the internal P/E series is inconsistent.** The data reports a trailing P/E of 28.53 alongside a forward P/E of 17.62–17.72 (dated 2026-05 to 2026-07). That is a very large gap — the forward multiple is roughly 38% below the trailing one — and the source material does not explain it. A gap of that size is normally driven either by an expected sharp earnings recovery or by a one-off hit to trailing earnings (the data separately notes a -31.4% net income decline, but does not state whether that decline is what depressed the trailing P/E's denominator, nor whether the forward estimate assumes a full rebound). Without that link being made explicit in the source, the forward P/E cannot be treated as a clean, like-for-like discount to the trailing figure — it may instead reflect analysts pricing in a recovery that hasn't happened yet. On the forward figure alone, Nestlé's 17.62–17.72 sits about 23% above the cited Consumer Packaged Goods peer-group median forward P/E of 14.32, which points toward "expensive relative to industry" on this one metric.

**The peer comparison is more ambiguous, and mixes trailing and forward figures.** Unilever's trailing P/E is given as 12.93 (said to be 28% below its own 10-year median, the lowest of the three), and Danone's trailing P/E is 22.54 (close to its own 10-year median, the highest of the three). Nestlé's forward P/E of 17.62–17.72 falls between these two numbers — but this is comparing Nestlé's *forward* multiple to peers' *trailing* multiples, an apples-to-oranges comparison that the source data does not reconcile. If Nestlé's own trailing P/E (28.53) were used instead, it would be the highest of the three, not the middle. So the "Nestlé sits in the middle of its peers" framing depends entirely on which of Nestlé's own two conflicting P/E figures is used, and the data gives no basis for picking one over the other.

**The DCF evidence is directly contradictory and cannot support a valuation conclusion.** One source puts fair value at $113.62 against a stated current price of $77.76 (implying ~46% upside), while another puts fair value at $179.01 against a stated current price of $102 (implying ~43% undervaluation). Beyond the fair-value estimates themselves diverging by roughly 58%, the two sources don't even agree on the current market price they are discounting from ($77.76 vs. $102) — a ~31% difference that is not explained anywhere in the data. This could stem from mixing the CHF-denominated Zurich-listed shares (NESN.SW) with the USD-denominated ADR (NSRGY), from different collection dates, or from some other methodological difference, but the source material explicitly does not clarify which. As a result, neither DCF output can be treated as reliable in isolation, and the two cannot be reconciled or averaged meaningfully.

**Compounding this, the underlying reference data itself is internally inconsistent in ways that bear directly on valuation.** Market capitalization is reported as both $258.56B and $385B (roughly a 1.5x discrepancy) with no stated cause, and the pricing throughout appears to mix CHF ordinary-share quotes with USD ADR quotes without any FX conversion supplied. Since P/E, DCF fair value, and market cap are all price-dependent, an unresolved currency and share-class mismatch of this kind undermines confidence in every multiple cited above, not just the DCF figures. The 86.9% payout ratio is also given without stating whether it is computed on a GAAP or adjusted earnings basis, or how it relates to the reported -31.4% net income decline — so it cannot be used to sanity-check the earnings figures either.

**Overall assessment:** On the single cleanest comparison available — forward P/E of 17.62–17.72 versus a peer-group median forward P/E of 14.32 — Nestlé appears to trade at a premium to its industry. However, this is the only data point in the set that is internally consistent enough to draw even a narrow conclusion from. The trailing-vs-forward P/E gap, the peer comparison's mixing of trailing and forward multiples, the two DCF models' both diverging fair values and diverging "current price" baselines, the market-cap discrepancy, and the unresolved CHF/USD mixing collectively mean that no aggregate "cheap vs. expensive" judgment can be responsibly drawn from this data set as a whole. The data is insufficient and internally inconsistent for that broader determination, and these specific contradictions — rather than any single multiple — are the main finding.

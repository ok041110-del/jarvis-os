## Nestlé S.A. (NESN.SW / NSRGY) — Valuation Analysis

On a pure multiples basis, Nestlé screens as moderately expensive relative to its own sector but roughly in line with, or cheaper than, one of its two closest packaged-food peers — while the discounted cash flow (DCF) evidence provided is too internally inconsistent to support any valuation conclusion at all.

**Multiples.** Nestlé's forward P/E of 17.62–17.72x sits 23.1% above the Consumer Packaged Goods industry median forward P/E of 14.32x, which on its face reads as a premium valuation for the sector. However, the peer comparisons included in the data complicate a simple "expensive" verdict: Unilever's trailing P/E of 12.93x is described as 28% below its own 10-year median (the cheapest of the group on that basis), while Danone's trailing P/E of 22.54x sits close to its 10-year median (the richest of the group). Nestlé's forward P/E of 17.62–17.72x falls between these two peers — cheaper than Danone, more expensive than Unilever. So whether Nestlé looks "cheap" or "expensive" depends heavily on which peer and which P/E convention (trailing vs. forward, current vs. historical-median-relative) is used as the benchmark; the data does not provide a single consistent basis for comparing all three companies on the same terms (e.g., Nestlé and Danone are given on a trailing basis in one place and Nestlé also on forward, while Unilever and Danone are anchored to their own 10-year medians rather than to the current industry median used for Nestlé).

**A first internal contradiction worth flagging directly:** Nestlé's own trailing P/E (28.53x) and forward P/E (17.62–17.72x) differ by a very large margin — roughly 38% — and the data does not explain why. A gap this size is typically driven by either an expected sharp earnings recovery or a prior-period earnings depression that a trailing multiple still reflects; the data separately notes a -31.4% net income decline, but does not state whether or how that decline is embedded in the trailing P/E, nor whether the forward P/E assumes a full reversal of it. Without that link being made explicit in the source data, the trailing/forward divergence cannot be interpreted with confidence — it could reflect a genuine, well-supported earnings rebound, or it could reflect an unusually depressed earnings base that overstates the multiple's true expensiveness. This should be treated as an open question, not resolved in either direction.

**DCF estimates are not usable as provided, due to a direct contradiction.** Two DCF-based fair value estimates are given, and they disagree not only on the answer but on the starting point:
- Source 1: fair value $113.62 vs. a stated current price of $77.76 (implying +46.1% upside)
- Source 2: fair value $179.01 vs. a stated current price of $102 (implying the market price is ~43% below fair value)

These two sources don't even agree on what the current share price is ($77.76 vs. $102), let alone the fair value ($113.62 vs. $179.01). The data explicitly notes it cannot determine whether this stems from mixing ADR (NSRGY, USD) pricing with ordinary share (NESN.SW, CHF) pricing, from different valuation dates, or from some other methodological difference between the two sources. Given that the "current price" inputs themselves are irreconcilable, neither DCF fair-value figure can be treated as a reliable read on intrinsic value relative to the market price — this is a genuine data contradiction, not a difference of analytical opinion, and it should not be averaged, split the difference on, or otherwise resolved without the missing information (currency basis, valuation date, and unit — ADR vs. ordinary share).

**Additional caveats that limit confidence in any of the above:**
- Market capitalization figures also conflict across sources by roughly 1.5x ($258.56B vs. $385B), with no stated explanation — this is consistent with, but not confirmed to be, the same currency/ADR-vs-ordinary-share issue affecting the DCF figures.
- No FX rate is provided anywhere in the data, so the CHF-denominated and USD-denominated figures cited across sections cannot be reconciled or cross-checked by a reader of this analysis.
- The 86.9% dividend payout ratio is given without its accounting basis (GAAP vs. adjusted) and without a stated link to the -31.4% net income decline, so it's unclear whether the elevated payout ratio reflects a deliberately maintained dividend against lower earnings or a different measurement basis.
- All figures are a point-in-time web-search snapshot as of 2026-08-16, not verified against Nestlé's primary half-year report or SIX exchange filings, and not live pricing.

**Bottom line on the data as given:** The multiples data alone would suggest Nestlé trades at a premium to the broad CPG industry median but within the range set by its two named peers, with its position "cheap" or "expensive" depending on which peer and which P/E convention is used — and the large gap between Nestlé's own trailing and forward P/E is left unexplained by the source data. The DCF evidence is unusable as a valuation signal because the two sources disagree on both the fair value and the current price used to compute it, most likely due to an unresolved currency or share-class mismatch. Given these contradictions and gaps, the data provided is insufficient to support a clear, single-direction judgment on whether Nestlé is cheap or expensive overall; a firmer read would require reconciling the currency/share-class basis across sources and understanding the driver of the trailing/forward P/E gap.

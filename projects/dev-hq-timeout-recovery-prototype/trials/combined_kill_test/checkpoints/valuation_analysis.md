## Nestlé S.A. (NESN.SW / NSRGY) — Valuation Read-Through

**On a straightforward P/E basis, Nestlé screens as expensive relative to its stated peer set.** The reported Forward P/E of 17.62–17.72 sits 23.1% above the cited Consumer Packaged Goods industry median Forward P/E of 14.32. Within the three-company peer comparison provided, Nestlé's forward multiple also lands between Unilever's trailing P/E of 12.93 (described as 28% below its own 10-year median — i.e., cheap on its own history) and Danone's trailing P/E of 22.54 (described as in line with its own 10-year median). So on this narrow peer set, Nestlé is priced richer than Unilever and cheaper than Danone — a "middle of the pack" read, though note this compares Nestlé's *forward* multiple against peers' *trailing* multiples, which is not an apples-to-apples comparison and the data doesn't supply Nestlé's trailing-to-peer or peer forward multiples to correct for that.

**A separate contradiction sits inside Nestlé's own numbers.** Trailing P/E (28.53) is roughly 62% higher than Forward P/E (17.62), implying the market expects a large jump in earnings. The data flags that net income reportedly fell -31.4%, but does not confirm whether this decline is what depressed the trailing multiple's earnings base, nor does it explain the forward re-rating. Without that link being made explicit in the source material, the size of the trailing/forward gap cannot be reliably interpreted — it could reflect a genuine expected earnings recovery, a one-off/non-recurring hit to trailing earnings, or something else entirely.

**The DCF evidence is not just noisy, it is internally contradictory and cannot support a valuation conclusion.** Two sources produce fair-value estimates that differ by more than 50% ($113.62 vs. $179.01), but more importantly, they don't even agree on the current price being used as the comparison baseline ($77.76 vs. $102) — a ~31% discrepancy in the "current price" input itself. Both sources report the stock as undervalued relative to their respective fair-value estimates (+46.1% and +43% respectively), so directionally they agree, but the underlying inputs are inconsistent enough that this agreement could be coincidental rather than corroborating. The data explicitly does not explain whether this stems from ADR (NSRGY, USD) vs. ordinary share (NESN.SW, CHF) price mixing, or from different snapshot dates — and no FX rate is provided to reconcile the two. Given this, the DCF figures should not be treated as a reliable cross-check on the multiples-based read.

**Additional consistency problems undermine confidence further.** Market capitalization is reported as both $258.56B and $385B across sources — a ~1.5x discrepancy with no stated cause — which by itself calls into question whether all valuation inputs in this dataset are drawn from a consistent price/share-count/currency basis. The dividend payout ratio (86.9%) is cited without clarifying whether it's GAAP or adjusted, or how it relates to the reported -31.4% net income decline, so it cannot be used to sanity-check earnings quality here.

**Net assessment:** On the one metric that is internally consistent and cleanly sourced — Forward P/E vs. stated industry median and named peers — Nestlé appears moderately expensive versus the CPG industry median and versus Unilever, though cheaper than Danone. That is a real signal, but a narrow one (three peers, single point-in-time). The DCF-based "cheap/expensive" signals should be treated as unreliable given the unreconciled currency/price-basis discrepancies within the source data itself, and the trailing-vs-forward P/E gap cannot be explained with what's provided. Given the market-cap discrepancy and unresolved currency mixing flagged in the source material, the data as given is insufficient to produce a confident, unified cheap/expensive judgment — the peer P/E comparison leans "expensive vs. industry," but this should not be read as a conclusion that overrides the unresolved contradictions in the DCF and price-basis data.

## Procter & Gamble (PG) — Dividend Quality Analysis

**Track record and growth.** The data indicates P&G has paid a dividend continuously for 136 years (since 1890) and has raised it for 70 consecutive years (since 1956), which would place it among the small group of "Dividend King" companies with multi-decade increase streaks. Over the most recent 10-year period, the dividend's compound annual growth rate is reported at 5%. Taken at face value, this points to a long, uninterrupted history of both payment and increases — one of the more well-established growth records among consumer staples dividend payers. I have not independently verified the 136-year and 70-year figures or the 5% CAGR against primary filings; they come from MacroTrends and other secondary aggregators cited in the source material, not from SEC filings directly reviewed here.

**Current dividend and minor data inconsistency.** The annual dividend is reported as $4.35/share in one part of the data and $4.36/share elsewhere — a small discrepancy that the source material itself acknowledges. The most recent quarterly payment is listed at $1.09/share, with an ex-dividend date of 2026-07-24 and a payment date of 2026-08-17, consistent with a quarterly payout cadence. The reported yield of approximately 3.02% is calculated off the $4.36 figure.

**Coverage — payout ratio.** The payout ratio is given as 63.77%. Relative to the peers referenced in the data, this sits in a middle range — higher than the 46.19% figure cited for JNJ, but lower than the 77.24%–80.1% range cited for KO. A payout ratio in the low-to-mid 60s (based on earnings) is generally a moderate level for a mature consumer-staples company, leaving some earnings retained beyond the dividend, though I cannot characterize it more precisely than "moderate" given the gaps below.

**Coverage — an important gap.** No free-cash-flow-based coverage ratio is provided in this data set, which is a meaningful limitation: payout ratio alone (typically an earnings-based measure) does not tell us whether the dividend is backed by actual cash generation, and FCF coverage is generally the more reliable test of dividend sustainability, particularly when a company reports one-off earnings volatility. The data itself flags a further ambiguity here: it notes that the 63.77% payout ratio coincides with a period in which quarterly GAAP EPS reportedly fell 15%, and it is not clear from the source whether the 63.77% figure is calculated on a trailing annual EPS basis or a more recent (and potentially distorted) quarterly basis. Because the data does not specify which, I cannot determine how much the reported payout ratio may be temporarily inflated or otherwise skewed by that EPS decline, nor can I assess how comfortably current cash flow covers both the existing dividend and the historical pace of annual increases.

**Overall assessment given the data provided.** The qualitative growth history (136 years of payments, 70 years of increases, 5% 10-year CAGR) is consistent with a dividend that has been durable over a very long horizon. However, the data provided is insufficient to fully assess *current* coverage strength: there is no FCF payout ratio, the earnings-based payout ratio's time basis (annual vs. a quarter affected by a reported EPS drop) is unclear, and even the basic annual dividend-per-share figure is inconsistently reported ($4.35 vs. $4.36) across sources. A confident judgment on how well current earnings/cash flow support the existing dividend and its historical growth pace would require FCF and free-cash-flow-payout data that this data set does not include, plus clarification of whether the payout ratio reflects trailing-twelve-month or single-quarter earnings.

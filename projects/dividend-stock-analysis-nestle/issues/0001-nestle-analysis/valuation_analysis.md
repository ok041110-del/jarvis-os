## Valuation Analysis: Nestlé S.A. (NESN.SW / NSRGY)

**On trailing earnings, Nestlé does not look cheap; on forward earnings, the picture is decidedly mixed, and the two multiples disagree with each other by a wide margin.** The trailing P/E of 28.53 is well above the forward P/E of 17.62–17.72, a gap the data does not explain — it is consistent with the market pricing in a rebound from depressed trailing earnings (the data separately notes net income fell 31.4%), but the source material never explicitly links these two facts, so this remains an inference rather than a stated conclusion. Because the earnings-decline figure and the P/E figures are not tied together in the source data, I cannot confirm that the trailing/forward gap is fully or even partially attributable to that decline.

**Relative to its immediate sector, Nestlé's forward multiple reads as moderately expensive.** At 17.62–17.72x forward earnings, Nestlé trades at a 23.1% premium to the Consumer Packaged Goods sector median forward P/E of 14.32. That alone would support a "not cheap" characterization on this specific metric.

**Relative to its direct peers, however, the signal is more ambiguous.** Nestlé's forward P/E sits between Unilever's trailing P/E of 12.93 (described as 28% below Unilever's own 10-year median — i.e., Unilever itself looks cheap on a historical basis) and Danone's trailing P/E of 22.54 (roughly in line with Danone's 10-year median). Note that these peer figures are trailing multiples while Nestlé's comparison point is forward — the data compares unlike bases, which weakens the peer read even before considering direction. Taken at face value, Nestlé is priced mid-pack between the cheapest (Unilever) and most fully-valued relative-to-its-own-history (Danone) peer, which is a neutral-to-slightly-elevated signal, not a clear "expensive" or "cheap" one.

**The DCF evidence is internally contradictory and should not be relied upon as presented.** Two sources produce fair-value estimates that differ by more than 50% ($113.62 vs. $179.01), and — critically — they don't even agree on what the current price is ($77.76 vs. $102). Both sources independently conclude the stock is undervalued (implying +46.1% and +43% upside respectively), but because their price bases differ by roughly 31%, it is not possible to determine whether these are two independent confirmations of undervaluation or two estimates built on inconsistent currency bases (CHF ordinary shares vs. USD ADR) or different collection dates that happen to both land on "undervalued" by coincidence. The data explicitly flags this ambiguity itself and does not resolve it, and neither can I from what's given.

**A further, unresolved contradiction: market capitalization figures differ by roughly 1.5x ($258.56B vs. $385B)** with no stated explanation. This is large enough that it is not attributable to normal rounding or minor timing differences, and it further undermines confidence in any single-number cross-source comparison (P/E, DCF, or otherwise) for this company in this dataset.

**Currency mixing is a systemic problem across the entire dataset, not just the DCF.** Some figures appear to reference the CHF-denominated Zurich-listed shares (NESN.SW) and others the USD-denominated ADR (NSRGY), and no FX rate is provided to reconcile them. Without that, none of the price-based comparisons in this data — DCF fair value vs. current price, or even the two "current price" figures against each other — can be verified as apples-to-apples.

**Bottom line on the data as given:** On sector-relative forward P/E, Nestlé screens moderately expensive (+23.1% to sector median). On peer P/E positioning, it screens as roughly mid-pack, neither clearly cheap nor clearly expensive, though the peer figures are trailing while Nestlé's is forward, which limits comparability. On DCF, both available estimates suggest undervaluation, but they are internally inconsistent with each other on both fair value and current price, and the currency/market-cap discrepancies elsewhere in the dataset raise real doubt about whether the DCF inputs are reliable. Given the unreconciled currency mixing, the ~1.5x market-cap discrepancy, and the two non-agreeing DCF bases, **the data as provided is not sufficient to support a clean, confident judgment of "cheap" or "expensive" overall** — it supports only the narrower, qualified observations above, each with the specific caveats noted. I have not filled in the missing FX rates, reconciled the price bases, or resolved the earnings-decline linkage, since none of that is present in the source material.

# Bear Case: iShares MSCI EAFE ETF (EFA)

## 1. Investors are paying an active-like price for a fund that can't prove passive-like precision

EFA charges a 0.32% expense ratio while using representative sampling rather than full replication — meaning it doesn't actually hold all ~700 index constituents, but a subset plus up to 20% in non-index or "economically similar" assets whose composition is undisclosed in the available data. The bear case here isn't that sampling is inherently bad — it's that **no EFA-specific tracking error figure exists in the data to justify the cost**. Investors are asked to trust that the methodology works, with only a third-party claim that a direct competitor (VEA) "generally" tracks tighter *and* charges less. When the cheaper, more precisely-tracking alternative to the same index exists, the burden of proof sits on EFA to show its 0.32% fee and sampling approach are earning their keep — and the provided data contains nothing that meets that burden.

## 2. A structural home-country bet dressed as diversification

EFA is sold as broad developed-ex-North-America exposure, but the data shows the top five countries account for over 60% of the fund, with Japan alone at ~24% — nearly a quarter of the entire portfolio riding on a single country's equity market and currency. Add the UK at ~15% and two countries alone approach 40% of the fund. An investor buying EFA for "international diversification" is, in practice, taking a concentrated macro bet on Japan and the UK specifically. The ~700-holding count is a diversification statistic that obscures this — breadth of *names* is not breadth of *country risk*, and the composition analysis itself flags that these two facts (broad name count vs. concentrated country weight) are never reconciled by the source data.

## 3. Unhedged, unquantified currency risk on top of that concentration

Every single analyst report — composition, holdings, performance/risk, and macro — independently flags the same gap: there is no data on whether EFA is currency-hedged, and no quantified FX sensitivity figures. Standard EAFE-index construction is unhedged, meaning a USD-based investor's returns are directly exposed to JPY, GBP, EUR, and CHF movements. Combined with the point above — a portfolio where Japan and UK alone are ~40% of assets — this means a large, undiversifiable slice of EFA's return is currency risk that the fund does nothing to manage, and that the available data cannot even size for prospective investors.

## 4. A documented history of severe drawdown risk

The since-inception maximum drawdown is 61.0% — a loss of well over half of peak capital at some point in the fund's history. This is a large, unambiguous number, not a discrepancy-riddled one, and it sits alongside a 20.8% annualized standard deviation as of 2026-08-14. A fund that has already demonstrated it can lose 61% of its value from peak to trough carries that tail risk as a standing structural feature, not a historical curiosity — and the recent calm one-year return of 20.06% does not offset that a similar-magnitude event has already occurred once in this fund's ~25-year life.

## 5. Sloppy, unreconciled reporting on the numbers that matter most to income and return

Across every single report, two core figures for this fund cannot be pinned down:
- **Dividend yield**: 3.36% vs. 2.75% — a 0.6-percentage-point gap, unexplained.
- **Since-inception annualized return**: 6.55% vs. 6.649%, from different sources, also unexplained.

For a fund an investor might select partly for its distribution characteristics, a 0.6-point unreconciled yield gap is not a trivial rounding issue — it represents meaningful uncertainty about what income the fund is actually generating today. This doesn't necessarily indict the fund itself, but it does mean an investor cannot currently get a clean, consistent read on EFA's basic yield and return characteristics from available sources, which should itself lower confidence in any specific numeric pitch for the fund.

## 6. No sector visibility — a blind spot, not a clean bill of health

None of the six reports contain sector-level weightings. It would be a mistake to assume this absence means sector risk is benign — it means sector concentration (e.g., overweight to financials or industrials, which the top-five holdings hint at with HSBC, Roche, Novartis, AstraZeneca skewing financials/healthcare) is simply unverifiable from what's available. An investor cannot rule out meaningful sector concentration risk sitting on top of the confirmed geographic concentration.

## 7. No macro tailwind identified to offset these risks

The macro report explicitly finds no data on eurozone, UK, or Japanese interest rates, growth, or inflation — the primary drivers of EFA's underlying markets. The only macro data point available (aggregate U.S. fund flows favoring ETFs generally) is not EFA-specific and cannot be used to infer any positive demand signal for this fund or its asset class. In other words, there is no evidence in the provided data of a macro tailwind that would justify accepting the concentration, currency, and drawdown risks above — the bull case, if one exists, is not supported by anything in this dataset.

---

### Summary
The strongest bear case for EFA, grounded strictly in the provided data, is not that it is a "bad" fund in some proven sense — the data doesn't support that conclusion either — but that it combines **(a)** a fee that a cheaper, reportedly better-tracking competitor undercuts, **(b)** a portfolio that is far more geographically concentrated (Japan/UK ~40%) than its "broad international" framing suggests, **(c)** fully unhedged and unquantified currency exposure layered on top of that concentration, **(d)** a demonstrated 61% historical max drawdown, and **(e)** materially inconsistent reporting on its own basic yield and return figures — all while no offsetting macro or sector data exists in the record to reassure a prospective holder. Where the data is silent (sector weights, hedging status, absolute tracking error, regional macro conditions), that silence itself is a risk an investor should price in, not a neutral fact to set aside.

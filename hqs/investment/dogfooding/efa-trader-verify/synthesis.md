**Where the two cases agree on facts.** The Bull and Bear cases share nearly identical underlying data points and diverge almost entirely in interpretation, not in the numbers themselves. Both agree on: the 0.32% expense ratio; ~700 holdings; top holdings and their weights (ASML 3.22%, HSBC 1.62%, Roche 1.39%, Novartis 1.32%, AstraZeneca 1.18%); country weights (~24% Japan, ~15% UK, ~9% France, ~9% Switzerland, Germany also top-five); the 61.0% since-inception maximum drawdown; the 20.06% one-year return; the unreconciled yield range (2.75%–3.36%) and unreconciled since-inception annualized return figures (6.55% vs. 6.649%); the absence of any EFA-specific tracking error figure (only a third-party claim that VEA "generally" tracks tighter and costs less); the absence of sector-level data; the absence of currency-hedging status or quantified FX sensitivity; and the fact that the $43.70B ETF-inflow figure is industry-wide, not EFA-specific. There is essentially no factual contradiction between the two cases — this is a rare case where both sides are working from the same evidentiary record.

**Where they diverge is interpretation.** The same 60%+ concentration in the top five countries is framed by the Bull as "targeted access to the world's most important developed economies outside North America" and by the Bear as "a structural home-country bet dressed as diversification." The same 700-name, low-single-stock-weight structure is framed by the Bull as genuine diversification and by the Bear as a statistic that obscures concentrated country and (unverifiable) sector risk. The same 0.32% fee is framed by the Bull as "known, contractual, disclosed cost" and by the Bear as an unjustified premium given a cheaper, reportedly better-tracking competitor exists. The Morningstar Silver rating is presented by the Bull as third-party validation; the Bear does not address it directly, which is itself a gap — it neither rebuts nor incorporates this data point. The 61% drawdown and 20.8% annualized standard deviation are acknowledged by both, but the Bull treats this as a qualification to be noted while the Bear treats it as a standing structural feature not offset by recent calm performance. The unhedged currency exposure is flagged as an "uncharacterized risk" by the Bull and as a compounding risk layered on top of geographic concentration by the Bear — same fact, different weight assigned.

**Open questions most likely to change the conclusion**, in rough order of leverage:
1. **EFA-specific tracking error and index-fidelity data** — without this, the core premise of the Bear's strongest argument (paying an active-like fee for unproven passive-like precision, with a cheaper alternative available) cannot be verified, nor can the Bull rebut it.
2. **Sector-level weightings** — currently a total blind spot; could confirm or dispel the Bear's suggestion of financials/healthcare concentration riding alongside the confirmed geographic concentration.
3. **Currency hedging status and quantified FX sensitivity** — both cases agree this is unknown; sizing it would materially change how much weight the Japan/UK concentration deserves.
4. **Reconciliation of the yield (2.75% vs 3.36%) and since-inception return (6.55% vs 6.649%) figures** — needed for any income- or long-run-return-based decision to rest on a single trustworthy number rather than a range.
5. **Regional macro conditions** (Japan, UK, eurozone rates/growth/inflation) — entirely absent from the data; this is the one category where the Bear explicitly notes there is no offsetting tailwind identified anywhere in the record, which if filled could either substantiate or undercut the concentration risk described in point 2 of the Bear case.

The dataset does not contain information sufficient to resolve any of these five questions, and neither case fabricates data to fill them — both explicitly flag the gaps rather than paper over them.

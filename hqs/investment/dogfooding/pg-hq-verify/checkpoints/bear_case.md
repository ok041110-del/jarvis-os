# BEAR CASE: Procter & Gamble (PG)

## 1. Earnings momentum is breaking down, and management is warning it gets worse before it gets better

The headline FY2026 numbers (net sales +3%, GAAP EPS +2%) mask a sharp deceleration in the most recent quarter. Q4 FY2026 GAAP diluted EPS fell **15% year-over-year** to $1.26, and Core EPS declined 3% to $1.43 — even as quarterly net sales still grew 2%. That means P&G is generating more revenue while converting meaningfully less of it to the bottom line, at least on a GAAP basis. Critically, management itself has flagged that **Q1 FY2027 EPS could decline more than 5% year-over-year** — this is not an outside analyst's bearish extrapolation, it is the company's own guidance. A company guiding to a second consecutive quarter of EPS decline is not a company with business momentum.

## 2. FY2027 guidance embeds a real chance of zero earnings growth

Full-year FY2027 Core EPS guidance is 0% to 3% growth off a $6.89 base — implying a range of $6.89 to $7.11. The low end of that range is **flat earnings, full stop**. This guidance is issued alongside a disclosed ~$1 billion after-tax headwind from commodities, energy, and transportation. Whether that $1B is already netted into the guidance range is not disclosed in the data — but either reading is bearish: if it's already included, it explains why the range's low end is zero growth; if it is not yet included, the guidance has real downside risk baked in that hasn't even been reflected yet. There is no version of this disclosure that supports a growth story for FY2027.

## 3. Structural cost actions signal a company trimming for survival, not investing for growth

P&G is running a two-year restructuring cutting **~15% of its non-manufacturing workforce (~7,000 people)**, with pre-tax restructuring charges estimated at **$1.0B–$1.6B**. That the program is running ahead of schedule (3,500+ of ~7,000 cuts already done at the one-year mark) could be read as efficient execution, but it can equally be read as a company front-loading defensive cost-cutting because the top-line isn't delivering enough on its own — consistent with the anemic 1% organic growth for the full year. Layered on top, the company is pushing **mid-single-digit price increases on 25% of its U.S. portfolio** starting August 2026 to offset tariff costs. The data provides no volume/elasticity information, so there is a live risk that price hikes erode unit volumes at exactly the moment organic growth is already weak (full-year organic growth of only 1%). Restructuring plus price hikes is a defensive playbook, not an offensive one.

## 4. Technical structure is the one clean, uncontaminated signal in the dataset — and it's bearish

Unlike nearly every other data point in this file, the technical picture is internally consistent and not contradicted by conflicting sources. As of the 2026-08-03 close, PG traded at $144.49, **below both its 20-day SMA ($148.06) and 50-day SMA ($147.33)** — a textbook weak/bearish structural setup. This stands in explicit contrast to peers JNJ and KO, which reportedly traded above all their moving averages with unanimous Strong Buy composite readings (12 buy/0 sell) in the same dataset. PG's own composite signal is a mixed 2 buy/2 sell Neutral — meaningfully worse, on a relative basis, than its direct staples peers. RSI at 42.4, below the 50 midpoint, adds a mild confirming tilt to the downside bias even though it isn't oversold. In a sector where the comparison set skews uniformly bullish, PG standing out as the technically weak name is notable.

## 5. Valuation is rich by at least one uncontested measure, with the possibility that it's much richer

Sources disagree sharply on PG's Forward P/E (21.1x vs. 25.4x), so the exact scale of overvaluation on an earnings basis can't be pinned down — but the bear case doesn't need that number to be resolved. The **Price-to-FCF comparison is clean and uncontradicted across sources: PG trades at 29.0x FCF vs. Unilever's 16.9x**, a ~1.7x premium. That premium is defended in the data only by qualitative appeals to PG's superior operating margin (24–26.3%, itself inconsistent) versus Unilever's ~17% — but no source provides any model or calculation translating that margin gap into a justified multiple. In other words, the premium is asserted, not demonstrated. Paying a clear, uncontested ~70% FCF-multiple premium to a key peer, without a quantitative justification, is a real valuation risk — especially layered on top of decelerating GAAP earnings and a company in defensive restructuring mode.

## 6. The dividend's coverage quality cannot be verified — and the one ratio available is trending the wrong direction indicator

The 136-year payment streak and 70-year increase streak are real and among the strongest in the market — this is not disputed. But sustainability going forward is a separate question from the historical record, and here the data is genuinely concerning rather than merely incomplete. The payout ratio of 63.77% is presented without clarity on whether it's calculated on an annual or quarterly EPS base — and this ambiguity lands squarely in a period where quarterly GAAP EPS just fell 15%. If the 63.77% figure reflects (or is skewed by) the weak Q4 print, coverage on a forward basis could be tighter than the headline number suggests. Compounding this, **no free-cash-flow-based dividend coverage ratio is available anywhere in the dataset** — for a capital-return story built on an uninterrupted multi-decade streak, the absence of the single most relevant metric (FCF coverage) at precisely the moment GAAP earnings are under pressure is a gap that should concern, not reassure, a prospective holder.

## 7. Even the bullish sentiment signal is too imprecise to lean on, and where it is precise, it's unflattering

Analyst ratings skew "Buy," but the target-price data behind that consensus is internally contradictory to a degree that undermines confidence in the whole apparatus: two sources citing the *same* "25 institutions" sample produce average targets of $163.30 and $178.63 — a ~9% spread that is unexplained. The low end of the disclosed target range, $145, implies essentially **flat-to-negative expected return (-0.85%)** from the estimated current price (~$146) — meaning even within the "Buy"-skewed consensus, a legitimate, cited data point sees no upside at all. Given that this same dataset shows systematic, unexplained discrepancies across nearly every quantitative figure (revenue: $87.0B vs $85.26B; tariff cost: $600M vs $1B; margins: 24% vs 26.3%), the qualitative "Buy" lean should be weighted lightly, while the concrete, low-end target of essentially zero upside stands as a legitimate independent data point that got buried by more optimistic headline averages.

---

### Summary of the bear thesis

Setting aside the data's numerous internal inconsistencies (which cut both ways and are not bear-specific), the facts that are clean, sourced, and hardest to explain away are: (1) a company-guided EPS decline into Q1 FY2027 following an already-weak Q4; (2) FY2027 Core EPS guidance whose low end is zero growth; (3) a workforce cut of 15% in non-manufacturing roles running concurrently with price hikes on a quarter of the U.S. portfolio — the profile of a company managing costs defensively rather than growing organically (full-year organic growth was just 1%); (4) a technical structure that is uniquely weak relative to sector peers in the same dataset; (5) an uncontested ~70% FCF-multiple premium to Unilever unsupported by any quantitative margin-to-multiple justification; and (6) a dividend payout ratio of uncertain basis, with FCF coverage entirely undisclosed, at exactly the moment GAAP EPS fell 15% in a single quarter. None of this requires resolving the report's numerous cross-source inconsistencies — the bear case stands on the subset of facts that are actually clean.
